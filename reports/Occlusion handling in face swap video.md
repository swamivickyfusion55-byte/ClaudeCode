# Ownership maps and look-ahead filters fix occlusion

The best practical answer on a 2-vCPU budget is not a new neural network. It is three cheap layers wrapped around the XSeg pass the pipeline already pays for. First, an **exclusive per-pixel ownership map** for touching faces: the front face's landmark region is removed from the behind face at full strength, each face's paste is clipped to its own outline, and the alphas sum to at most 1. Second, a **flow-warped, look-ahead temporal filter on the occluder probability**: accept high-confidence occluders on the frame they appear, use a centred median of frames t−1, t and t+1 to reject one-frame blips, then a short peak-hold release and hysteresis. Third, **tracking that survives obstruction**: low-score re-association, landmark carry on visible points only, interpolation across the look-ahead window, and a roughly 1-second hold during which the swap keeps painting. Together these cost single-digit milliseconds per face; the timed pieces were **[measured]** at 0.1–1.7 ms each on a research bench. The binding cost is XSeg itself, about **142 ms per face-frame [measured, production log]**, and that is already spent. So extending it from the mouth/chin zone to the whole face costs no inference time. FaceFusion and Deep-Live-Cam apply no temporal filter to their occlusion masks and have no "other face" exclusion. No paper addresses front/behind ordering of two faces in contact. The two-face design below is therefore engineering inference built on sourced components, not a published method. Heavier tools stay off the per-frame path: EdgeSAM instance segmentation, BiSeNet parsing, depth models and the three-model XSeg ensemble each cost 0.2–0.9 s or more. They are justified only as contact-frame escalations, if at all. Evidence labels used throughout: **[measured]** = timed on real hardware (bench or production log), **[sourced]** = stated in a cited source, **[unverified]** = estimate or background knowledge not confirmed this session.

## XSeg already consumes most of the budget, so every addition must be millisecond-class

The research separates occlusion methods into two families. Only one of them fits an inswapper_128 pipeline. FaceShifter's HEAR-Net learns occlusions from "heuristic error", the gap between a face and its own reconstruction by the swapper, and needs no occlusion labels. It is tied to its AEI-Net generator, though, and no official weights exist ([arXiv 1912.13457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.13457v3)); that weights claim is **[unverified]**. FaceDancer keeps target occlusions through attention fusion inside its own generator ([arXiv 2210.10473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10473v1)). Every production tool instead uses **segmentation-gated compositing**: a visible-face mask decides where the swap is pasted. This family includes DeepFaceLab's XSeg, FaceFusion, FSGAN's segmentation-plus-inpainting stack ([fsgan](https://github.com/YuvalNirkin/fsgan)) and E4S's parser masks ([E4S](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.15081)). A 2024 survey still calls extreme occlusion unsolved for swap methods ([survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17881)).

FaceFusion 3.9.2 is the closest reference implementation, and its design is instructive in what it does and does not do. Its occlusion mask is XSeg run on the **pre-swap target crop**, so hands and cups are segmented in the real frame and never covered by a hallucinated face. The mask is post-processed as `(GaussianBlur(m, σ=5).clip(0.5,1) − 0.5)·2` and intersected with other masks by pixel-wise `minimum` ([face_masker.py @3.9.2](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion/blob/3.9.2/facefusion/face_masker.py)). It keeps **no temporal state for masks**. Multiple faces are pasted sequentially, each crop taken from the already-modified frame, with no inter-face exclusion ([face_swapper/core.py @3.9.2](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion/blob/3.9.2/facefusion/processors/modules/face_swapper/core.py)). Its 3.7.0 tracker only interpolates missing detections across a ±2-frame window ([face_tracker.py @3.9.2](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion/blob/3.9.2/facefusion/face_tracker.py)). Deep-Live-Cam likewise has no temporal occluder logic, and users have filed "flickering face mask" reports there ([Deep-Live-Cam #1610](https://github.com/hacksider/Deep-Live-Cam/issues/1610)). DeepFaceLive skips XSeg at runtime because iperov judged generic XSeg too slow for real time ([DeepFaceLive #63](https://github.com/iperov/DeepFaceLive/issues/63)). The pipeline under study is therefore already past the open-source state of the art on occlusion. The gains left are in temporal behaviour and instance separation, not in a better per-frame occluder.

Cost is what decides the design. On a 4-thread 2.1 GHz Xeon with onnxruntime 1.30, one XSeg pass took **294–352 ms**, BiSeNet-ResNet18 at 512 px took **231 ms**, and BiSeNet-ResNet34 took **390 ms** **[measured, research bench]** ([models](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion-assets/releases/tag/models-3.1.0)). The deployed engine logs **142 ms per face-frame** for xseg_2 on the Space's 2 CPUs **[measured, production log]** (local: `/home/user/ClaudeCode/SDOS-091_OcclusionNet.md`). The two measurements disagree by about 2×, and the cause (CPU model, thread settings, or how the log timer is scoped) is unknown. Use the production figure for budgeting, and re-time it before committing. One research note estimated XSeg at 10–25 ms and BiSeNet at 60–120 ms on 2 cores. Both bench measurements contradict those figures, so discard them. The consequence is blunt. XSeg alone uses half or more of the 0.1–0.3 s allowance. FaceFusion's `many` ensemble (three XSeg passes combined by minimum, about 0.9 s) and per-frame BiSeNet are out of budget. Everything added per frame must be classical image processing in the 1–5 ms class. The research bench timed the relevant OpenCV primitives on the same Xeon **[measured]** at 256 px, 2 threads. **DIS ULTRAFAST optical flow** takes **0.59 ms**, a float `remap` 0.07 ms, a 7×7 open plus connected components 0.26 ms, and pyramidal Lucas-Kanade on 68 points 0.62 ms.

## Touching faces need an exclusive ownership map, not a better occluder

No public model separates "this face" from "the other person's face". BiSeNet trained on CelebAMask-HQ has 19 classes and no hand, object or other-person class, so a partner's cheek inside face A's crop is simply labelled skin ([yakhyo/face-parsing](https://github.com/yakhyo/face-parsing)); the label list itself is **[unverified]**. Occlusion segmenters such as the NatOcc/RandOcc models are binary, visible face versus everything else ([Voo et al., CVPRW 2022](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022W/VDU/html/Voo_Delving_Into_High-Quality_Synthetic_Face_Occlusion_Segmentation_Datasets_CVPRW_2022_paper.html)). XSeg's training composites foreign backgrounds into 50% of samples ([SampleGeneratorFaceXSeg.py](https://github.com/iperov/DeepFaceLab/blob/master/samplelib/SampleGeneratorFaceXSeg.py)). That teaches it "outside the polygon is not face", but not "another face is not this face". The only local evidence is a synthetic cheek-to-cheek clip, where XSeg cut about 2.5% of the crop during contact frames **[measured, synthetic, mouth/chin zone only]** (local: `SDOS-091_OcclusionNet.md`). XSeg can therefore contribute a vote but cannot own the decision. DeepFaceLab's answer was manual exclude-polygons per project ([DFL XSegEditor commit](https://github.com/iperov/DeepFaceLab/commit/01d81674fdf0acb1b896db3b984828c9812d9111)), which an automated pipeline cannot use.

The current engine already has the right primitive, but three details let one face paint over the other (local: `/home/user/ClaudeCode/v11.2.94_Natural/swap_engine.py`, `core_pipeline.py`). The rival cut removes only **85%** of a front face's landmark hull from the behind face (`rival_cut = 0.85`), with a wide blur of 9% of the crop (`rival_feather = 0.09`). The remaining 15% of the behind face's swap is painted over the front person. The template oval is floored at **30%** outside the face's own hull (`hull_floor = 0.30`). The front face, which has no rivals, therefore still paints at 30% or more onto the behind face wherever its oval reaches beyond its jaw. The rival cut also passes through the symmetric mask EMA (`mask_ema = 0.36`). By my arithmetic, a cut that appears in one frame reaches only about 36%, 59% and 74% of full strength over its first three frames, so contact edges trail the motion. Front/behind ordering is decided by bounding-box area alone, which is weak in a kiss, where both faces are about the same size.

The recommended design is a **per-pixel ownership map in frame space**. It works with no new model and costs an estimated **under 5 ms [unverified]**. Compute every face's soft visibility mask from the **original** frame, not the partly composited one, which avoids FaceFusion's sequential-crop flaw. Clip each face's mask to its own landmark support, extended to the forehead, and feather it only inward so no face's alpha leaves its own outline. In the overlap of two supports, give the pixels to the front face and remove them from the behind face at **full strength**, with a narrow edge rather than a 9% blur. Finally, composite as `out = frame·(1 − Σmᵢ) + Σ mᵢ·swapᵢ`, normalising wherever the alphas sum above 1, so no pixel can receive two swaps. Apply this rival exclusion **after** the temporal mask smoothing, exactly as the engine already does for the occlusion net. The ownership decision itself is held stable by hysteresis on the order, not by blurring the mask. With exclusive masks, paint order no longer matters, and each face keeps a distinct outline even when the two touch.

Ordering should be a **vote with hysteresis**, run only when the expanded boxes or hulls overlap. No published method or dataset exists for this case, so every cue below is reasoned, not validated. The strongest cue for kisses and cheek-to-cheek is **landmark intrusion**: the face whose landmarks fall inside the other face's exclusive visible region is behind, because its regressor hallucinated points under the occluder. The second is an **XSeg cross-check**: if XSeg in B's crop flags the overlap as an occluder while XSeg in A's crop calls it face, A is in front, and since both crops' XSeg outputs already exist this costs nothing extra. Weaker votes come from **landmark jitter** (occluded points jitter more after a similarity fit) and **scale** (inter-ocular distance, useful only when one person is clearly nearer the camera). Switch the order only after 3–5 consistent frames; these thresholds are **[unverified]** suggestions. Two kissing profiles are where landmark hulls are crudest. For them, the escalation is a promptable segmenter run **only on contact frames**, on the union crop of the two faces. Prompt it with each face's box and landmarks, and add negative points on the other face. EdgeSAM is the lightest candidate with ONNX encoder/decoder exports: **22.1 GFLOPs** against MobileSAM's 38.2 **[sourced]** ([EdgeSAM](https://github.com/chongzhou96/EdgeSAM)). MobileSAM's own README reports about **3 s per image on a Mac i5 CPU** **[sourced]** ([MobileSAM](https://github.com/ChaoningZhang/MobileSAM)). EdgeSAM on 2 x86 cores is estimated at 0.3–0.8 s **[unverified]**, so it must be amortised: run it every 2–4 contact frames and carry its masks by landmark affine in between. Person instance segmentation is cheaper but coarse. YOLO26n-seg runs at **53.3 ms CPU ONNX** on an unnamed CPU **[sourced]** ([Ultralytics](https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics)). It separates people, not faces, so it helps only as a body-level ownership prior in hugs. Monocular depth (Depth Anything V2-Small) has no published x86 CPU latency; the nearest figure is **77.3 ms on a Snapdragon X Elite** **[sourced]** ([Qualcomm](https://huggingface.co/qualcomm/Depth-Anything-V2)). Whether relative depth resolves the few centimetres at a kiss boundary is unknown, so treat it as a last-resort tie-breaker.

## A confidence-split look-ahead filter removes occluders on the same frame and releases them cleanly

The occluder requirement pulls in two directions. A hand must be cut on the frame it arrives, yet a borderline pixel must not chatter. Persistence counters delay real occluders by N−1 frames, and symmetric EMAs both lag and ghost. Deep-Live-Cam's α = 0.2 frame blend delays an occluder by roughly 4–5 frames ([Deep-Live-Cam](https://github.com/hacksider/Deep-Live-Cam/tree/main/modules/processors/frame)). The chunked pipeline's look-ahead resolves this, at about **2–3 ms per face**; the primitives are **[measured]**, the total is an **[unverified]** estimate. Work on the **soft occluder probability** `o = 1 − xseg` in 256-px aligned-crop space. Start by **motion-compensating**: warp the previous state and the t+1 probability into frame t's crop, first through the relative alignment affine and then through DIS ULTRAFAST flow (0.59 ms). The **attack** is the per-pixel median of `o(t−1)`, `o(t)` and `o(t+1)`: an occluder lasting at least two frames gives `median(0,1,1) = 1` and is accepted on its first frame, while a one-frame false positive gives `median(0,1,0) = 0` and is rejected. The median would also reject a genuine one-frame object, such as a motion-blurred hand swipe, so add a **confidence split** of my own devising: bypass the median where `o(t)` is about 0.8 or above, and use it only in the ambiguous band. The **release** is a peak-hold envelope, `S(t) = max(o′(t), d·S_warped(t−1))`. Wherever a hard decision is needed, apply **hysteresis** (on above 0.6, off only after 2 frames below 0.3) and paste through a smoothstep rather than a 0.5 cut. A **spatial filter** then drops components smaller than about 0.5–1% of the crop unless they touch the support border, because real occluders enter from outside the face. Finally, **reset** safely: skip warping when forward-backward flow error is high, and clear all state at a shot cut, which the look-ahead detects one frame early. A VOS anti-chatter patent warps neighbouring masks, averages the soft values, then thresholds, which supports this soft-vote design ([US 8358691](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8358691)). The specific constants are **[unverified]** and need tuning on the project's contact and object clips.

The release constant carries a real trade-off. A slow decay (d ≈ 0.6–0.75, which takes 3–6 frames to fade) suppresses edge chatter. It also leaves a short trail of *original* face where the hand used to be, a partial revert of exactly the kind requirement (2) forbids. Flow-warping the envelope keeps that trail attached to the moving object rather than stamped on the cheek. Applying the decay only within a few pixels of the occluder boundary would keep the bulk of the vacated area returning on the next frame. My recommendation is a shorter decay, **d ≈ 0.5** (2–3 frames), restricted to the boundary band, rather than the 3–6 frames suggested in the notes.

Coverage is the other lever, and it is free. The engine's XSeg already runs once per crop over the whole 256-px face, and that output is shared with the profile mask (local: `swap_engine.py`, `occ_net_raw`). Yet only components reaching the mouth/chin zone are cut, because hair over a cheek once removed up to 3.7% of a long-haired face (local: `SDOS-091_OcclusionNet.md`). The new requirement explicitly lists hair and hands over any part of the face. So extend the cut to the full landmark hull, eroded about 8% so the hairline and jaw outline are never re-shaped. Keep the component rule "must enter from outside the support", and let the temporal filter absorb the boundary flicker that motivated the zone limit. XSeg's known blind spots are mechanism-based expectations, not reported failures. Beards, stubble and glasses sit at the edge of XSeg's notion of "face" ([analysis of face_masker.py](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion/blob/3.9.2/facefusion/face_masker.py)), so validate on those before shipping.

Clean edges come from treating the two boundary types differently. The **swap boundary** (jaw, hairline) needs a wide feather to hide colour mismatch. The **occluder or rival boundary** has a hard physical edge. Give it a narrow feather, a 2–4 px dilation of the occluder before feathering so swapped pixels never bleed onto fingers, and an edge-aware snap. A guided filter (`cv2.ximgproc.guidedFilter`) with the target crop as guide runs in O(N) with box filters. It costs an estimated **2–5 ms per face [unverified]**, and its complexity claim is from background knowledge. FaceFusion's fixed σ = 5 blur is about 4× wider relative to the face at a 128 crop than at 512 ([face_masker.py @3.9.2](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion/blob/3.9.2/facefusion/face_masker.py)). So scale any feather to face width, not to fixed pixels. Learned matting (RVM, MODNet) produces person-level alpha with GPU-only published speeds ([RVM](https://github.com/PeterL1n/RobustVideoMatting)), so it does not fit here.

## Tracks, not detections, keep the swap painted through obstruction

Reverting to the original face is a geometry failure, not a mask failure. The occluder mask already hides the swap where the face is covered, so the swap should keep painting at the best available geometry whenever a track is alive. The tracking literature gives sourced defaults. **ByteTrack** re-associates low-score detections (0.1 < score < 0.5) against unmatched tracks in a second pass and keeps lost tracks for **30 frames**, scaled by frame rate ([ByteTrack](https://github.com/ifzhang/ByteTrack/blob/main/yolox/tracker/byte_tracker.py)). **OC-SORT** defaults to `max_age = 30` and adds two mechanisms ([OC-SORT](https://github.com/noahcao/OC_SORT)). The first rebuilds the lost interval by linear interpolation once a track is re-found. The second matches unmatched detections against each track's *last observation*. FaceFusion's tracker likewise interpolates bounding boxes and landmarks between anchor detections inside its window ([face_tracker.py @3.9.2](https://github.com/facefusion/facefusion/blob/3.9.2/facefusion/face_tracker.py)).

For this pipeline, the recipe is as follows. Accept low-score SCRFD hits for existing tracks only. When a large share of the face is occluded, about 30–40% or more, stop trusting fresh landmarks: carry the previous set with Lucas-Kanade on visible points (0.62 ms **[measured]**), reject points whose forward-backward error is high or that sit on the occluder mask, and fit a RANSAC similarity transform. If the track is re-detected inside the chunk, replace the predictions with interpolation between anchors. Hold for about 1 second, then fade.

Smooth the 4-DOF alignment transform with a centred [0.25, 0.5, 0.25] kernel over t−1..t+1. This is zero-phase, so it beats a causal 1€ filter for offline chunks. The 1€ reference defaults are mincutoff 1 Hz, β 0.1 and dcutoff 1 Hz **[sourced]** ([OneEuroFilter](https://github.com/casiez/OneEuroFilter)). Leave mouth landmarks lightly smoothed so lip motion does not lag.

During contact, gate identity re-matching on embeddings from frames with less than about 20% occlusion. OC-SORT can swap identities when objects cross for many frames ([Labellerr](https://www.labellerr.com/blog/oc-sort/)), so embeddings are the guard. Never gate the whole-face swap on an occlusion fraction; occlusion must act only per pixel. These thresholds are **[unverified]** suggestions. The engine already has a crossing lock that freezes embeddings while two tracks overlap (local: `core_pipeline.py`), which matches this guidance.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Better segmentation models are not the bottleneck: the occluder the pipeline already runs is the same one FaceFusion ships. What is missing is the plumbing around it. That means temporal decisions that use the look-ahead the chunked design already provides, and an ownership rule that makes it physically impossible for two swaps to share a pixel. The most consequential near-term change in the current engine is small. Raise the rival cut to full strength with a narrow edge, stop the 30% template floor from spilling outside a face's own outline, and keep the rival cut out of the lagging symmetric EMA. Together these close the "paints over the other" path in requirement (1) for well under a millisecond.

Two open questions decide whether anything heavier is ever needed. The first is how often landmark-hull ownership fails on kissing profiles. The second is whether the release trail of the temporal filter reads as a revert. Both are empirical. They should be answered on the project's own contact and object clips (task #6) before any money is spent on EdgeSAM or depth. The 2× gap between the bench and production XSeg timings should be resolved at the same time, because it decides whether a second model on contact frames fits at all.
